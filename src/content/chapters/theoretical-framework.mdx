## 2.2 Intention to participate

The dependent variable of this study is guests' intention to participate in hotel sustainability practices. According to the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), intention is the most important direct predictor of behaviour and shows how hard people are willing to try to perform a behaviour (Ajzen, 1991). Intention is in turn shaped by a person's attitude, the social pressure they perceive (subjective norm) and how easy or difficult they think the behaviour is (perceived behavioural control).

TPB is widely used in hospitality research. Han et al. (2010) showed that attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control all explain customers' intention to visit a green hotel, and Chen and Tung (2014) found that adding environmental concern to the model improves its explanatory power. Most relevant for this study, Budovska et al. (2020) applied TPB to towel reuse among 438 respondents and found that attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control positively affected hotel guests' intention to reuse a towel. For these reasons, this study uses an extended TPB model as its theoretical framework. It measures intention rather than actual behaviour, because a survey cannot observe what guests really do during their stay. This is a limitation, as intentions do not always turn into action (Kollmuss & Agyeman, 2002).

## 2.3 Environmental concern

Environmental concern refers to the degree to which people are aware of environmental problems and support efforts to solve them (Dunlap & Jones, 2002). It is often measured with the New Ecological Paradigm scale (Dunlap et al., 2000). According to the value–belief–norm theory, people who care about the environment feel a personal obligation to act in an environmentally friendly way (Stern, 2000). In the hotel context, Chen and Tung (2014) found that environmental concern positively influences consumers' attitudes and their intention to visit green hotels.

However, the effect of environmental concern in a holiday setting is not straightforward. Miao and Wei (2013) found that people act less pro-environmentally in hotels than at home, because on holiday hedonic motives such as comfort and enjoyment become more important. Similarly, Juvan and Dolnicar (2014) showed that even tourists who are active environmentalists at home often do not behave sustainably when they travel. In a field experiment in a Slovenian hotel, messages that appealed to guests' environmental values did not significantly increase towel reuse or reduce electricity use (Dolnicar et al., 2017). Environmental concern may therefore be a necessary but not a sufficient condition. Still, based on the theory, a positive relationship with intention is expected, and testing its strength in a Dutch hotel context is one of the contributions of this study:

<Hypothesis id="H1">Environmental concern is positively associated with hotel guests' intention to participate in hotel sustainability practices.</Hypothesis>

## 2.4 Social norms

Social norms are the unwritten rules about how people behave in a group. Cialdini et al. (1990) distinguish between descriptive norms (what most people actually do) and injunctive norms (what most people approve of). In TPB, the related concept is the subjective norm: the social pressure that a person feels to perform a behaviour (Ajzen, 1991).

Social norms are one of the most studied factors in hotel sustainability research. In a well-known field experiment, Goldstein et al. (2008) showed that a card telling guests that most other guests reused their towels (a descriptive norm) increased towel reuse to 44%, compared with 35% for the standard environmental message. When the message referred to guests who had stayed in the same room, reuse rose to 49%. Terrier and Marfaing (2015) confirmed that normative messages, as well as a small commitment by the guest, increase towel reuse, although combining both techniques did not give an extra effect. Baca-Motes et al. (2013) also found that a simple commitment made at check-in increased pro-environmental behaviour during the stay. Budovska et al. (2020) showed that subjective norm is a significant predictor of the intention to reuse towels, but in their scenario study the specific type of normative message did not make a difference. Taken together, these studies suggest that guests who believe that other people participate, or expect them to participate, are more willing to do so themselves:

<Hypothesis id="H2">Social norms are positively associated with hotel guests' intention to participate in hotel sustainability practices.</Hypothesis>

## 2.5 Perceived convenience

Perceived convenience is defined in this study as the extent to which guests believe that taking part in a sustainability practice is easy and requires little time or effort. It is closely related to perceived behavioural control in TPB (Ajzen, 1991). Kollmuss and Agyeman (2002) argue that practical barriers such as inconvenience and missing facilities are among the main reasons why pro-environmental attitudes do not lead to behaviour. In the hotel context, Budovska et al. (2020) found that perceived behavioural control positively influences the intention to reuse towels.

The way a hotel designs the choice also matters. According to nudge theory, small changes in the choice environment, such as changing the default option, can steer behaviour without limiting freedom of choice (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). Knezevic Cvelbar et al. (2021) demonstrated this in a Slovenian hotel: when room cleaning changed from automatic (opt-out) to on request (opt-in), the share of rooms that were cleaned fell from about 89% to 32%.

This shows that making the sustainable option the easiest option can strongly increase participation. It is therefore expected that:

<Hypothesis id="H3">Perceived convenience is positively associated with hotel guests' intention to participate in hotel sustainability practices.</Hypothesis>

## 2.6 Perceived personal benefits

Perceived personal benefits (incentives and rewards) are the advantages that guests expect to receive when they participate, such as a voucher, loyalty points, a discount, or the good feeling of contributing. Several studies suggest that tangible benefits work better than moral appeals in a hotel setting. Dolnicar et al. (2019) compared three approaches in a four-star hotel: an environmental message, an equity-based offer (a free drink voucher for every skipped room clean) and a combination of both. The equity-based approach, in which the hotel shares its savings with the guest, reduced room cleaning by 42% and was clearly more effective than the environmental message alone. The authors conclude that hotels should use messages that signal fairness and the sharing of benefits between hotel and guest.

Dimara et al. (2017) found in Greece that around 72% of hotel customers would take part in a towel reuse programme, but that willingness to contribute differs between guests, which suggests that the perceived value of participating matters. A possible risk is that rewards replace intrinsic motivation, so guests only participate as long as they are paid for it. This will be explored further in the interviews. Based on the reviewed literature, it is expected that:

<Hypothesis id="H4">Perceived personal benefits are positively associated with hotel guests' intention to participate in hotel sustainability practices.</Hypothesis>

## 2.7 Guest characteristics

Guest characteristics may also explain differences in participation. Knezevic Cvelbar et al. (2017) found that towel reuse differs between hotel guest segments, and they recommend targeted marketing instead of one message for all guests. Dimara et al. (2017) reported that younger guests, guests in premium rooms and guests with longer stays were more willing to financially support towel reuse programmes, and Knezevic Cvelbar et al. (2021) found that leisure guests on longer stays responded differently from short-stay guests. Therefore, gender, age, travel purpose and length of stay are included as demographic variables. Differences between groups will be tested with an independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA (SQ2), without formulating separate hypotheses.
