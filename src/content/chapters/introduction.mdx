## 1.1 Research context

Sustainability has become a central issue for the hospitality industry. Hotels use large amounts of energy, water and cleaning chemicals, and a large part of this consumption is caused by guest-related services such as daily room cleaning and laundry. For example, one routine room clean uses around 1.5 kWh of electricity, 35 litres of water and 100 ml of chemicals (Knezevic Cvelbar et al., 2021). At industry level, the World Sustainable Hospitality Alliance (2017) calculated that hotels must reduce their absolute carbon emissions by 66% by 2030 and by 90% by 2050 to stay in line with the Paris Climate Agreement.

At the same time, travellers say that sustainability matters to them. In Booking.com's 2025 Travel & Sustainability research among 32,000 travellers in 34 countries and territories, 84% said that sustainable travel is important to them and 93% said they want to make more sustainable choices when they travel (Booking.com, 2025). Many hotels have responded by introducing initiatives that depend on the guest, such as towel and linen reuse programmes, the option to skip daily housekeeping, and waste separation in guest rooms.

## 1.2 Problem statement

Hotels in the Netherlands increasingly invest in sustainability initiatives such as towel and linen reuse programmes, reduced (opt-out) housekeeping services and waste-sorting systems to minimise their environmental footprint. These initiatives only work when guests actually take part. In practice, however, guest participation often remains low. Field studies show that when hotels use a standard environmental message, only around one third of guests reuse their towels (Goldstein et al., 2008), and appeals to guests' environmental values alone do not significantly change their behaviour (Dolnicar et al., 2017). This creates a clear gap between the desired performance of these programmes and their actual performance: hotels invest in sustainability, but the expected savings in water, energy, chemicals and costs are not fully achieved.

<BarChart id="goldstein" figure="2" />

The problem is urgent because the hospitality sector is under pressure to decarbonise quickly (World Sustainable Hospitality Alliance, 2017), while travellers' positive attitudes towards sustainability do not automatically turn into sustainable behaviour during a hotel stay. This so-called attitude–behaviour gap is well documented in tourism (Juvan & Dolnicar, 2014) and people tend to behave less environmentally friendly in a hotel than at home (Miao & Wei, 2013). It is still unclear which factors influence guests' willingness to engage in sustainable practices during their hotel stay. As a result, many hotel managers rely on generic "save the planet" cards instead of evidence-based communication, behavioural nudges or reward programmes.

<Scope>

This research focuses on adult guests (18+) who have stayed in a hotel in the Netherlands in the past 12 months, for leisure or business. It looks at three guest-dependent practices:

- reusing towels and bed linen,
- skipping daily housekeeping, and
- sorting waste in the room.

Hotel-side measures without guest involvement (e.g., solar panels, building insulation) are outside the scope.

</Scope>

## 1.3 Research objective

The objective of this research is to identify the factors that influence hotel guests' intention to participate in hotels' sustainability practices in the Netherlands, in order to provide hotel managers with evidence-based and implementable communication strategies, behavioural nudges and reward programmes that increase guest participation.

## 1.4 Research questions

<MainQuestion>
To what extent do environmental concern, social norms, perceived convenience and perceived personal benefits influence hotel guests' intention to participate in hotels' sustainability practices in the Netherlands?

## 1.5 Research significance

**Practical relevance.** The results will help hotel managers decide where to invest: in better communication, in making participation easier, or in rewards. The final recommendations will include a concrete action plan, time plan and budget estimate.

**Theoretical relevance.** Most field evidence on hotel guest behaviour comes from other countries, such as the United States (Goldstein et al., 2008), Slovenia (Dolnicar et al., 2017, 2019), and Greece (Dimara et al., 2017). This study tests an extended Theory of Planned Behaviour model among hotel guests in the Netherlands.
